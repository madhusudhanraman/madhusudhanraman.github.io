All fiction is deceitful. It asks boldly --- unreasonably, even ---
that we suspend our acute knowledge of its counterfeit nature. That it
succeeds in inspiring in us joy, or sorrow, or anything at all is, well,
rather miraculous. The artists' legerdemain is in constructing for us
so plausible and complete a picture of their fictional world that
our anxieties about its unreality are quietened, or set aside
temporarily. There are rare moments when this sleight of hand is so
perfect that it disappears entirely; then art becomes a perfect mirror
of reality, reaching out from the modest boundaries of its medium to
swallow the world whole. 

Edward Yang's _Yi Yi_ does just this.

To say that _Yi Yi_ follows roughly a year in the lives of a Taiwanese
family is true, but this is the truth of accountants. It seems to me
that to attempt a summary of its plot is a fool's errand, or rather,
it is to miss the point of the film entirely. (Of the meticulous
stories of Julio Cortázar, Borges says something similar: "If we try
to summarise them, we realise that something precious has been lost.")
Yang's characters --- lugubrious, impassive --- may as well be us;
their abortive loves, minor tragedies, and obscure resignations,
neatly bookended by a wedding and a funeral, may as well be ours.

At the film's close, each character's arc is perfectly balanced. Each
shot is composed with delicate and loving attention to detail. This
must have been a difficult film to make. How did Yang do it? I like to
think one of the minor characters, Mr. Ota (played masterfully by
Issey Ogata) tells us. He shows NJ (We Nien-jen) a card trick he
learned as a boy, and when prompted to explain how he did it,
clarifies that there is no magic at all; he just knows where all the
cards in the deck are. 

There are no tricks. I believe this, sincerely. Although I suspect,
when I revisit this film, I will still look keenly for them.
