Borges refers to the work of Ramon Llull (or Raymond Lully, or even
Raimundo Lulio) at least thrice in his oeuvre. My earliest encounter
with this medieval ecclesiastic was in _Pierre Menard, Author of the
Quixote_, where Borges, after presenting us with the endorsements he
has obtained from the Baroness de Bacourt and the Countess de
Bagnoregio, proceeds to enumerate the "visible" work left behind by
the titular novelist.[^1] Among these, we find:

> ...\
> f) A monograph on Raymond Lully's _Ars magna generalis_ (Nîmes,
> 1906).\
> ...

This reference, one of nineteen, is of no great significance; the list
serves to orient us, to help better understand the peculiarities of
Menard's thought by illuminating his intellectual milieu. A more
substantive reference to Llull appears in _A Note on (toward) Bernard
Shaw_, where Borges writes:[^2]

> At the end of the thirteenth century, Raymond Lully (Raimundo Lulio)
> was prepared to solve all arcana by means of an apparatus of
> concentric, revolving discs of different sizes, subdivided into
> sectors with Latin words...
 
He discusses this apparatus in more detail in a short essay titled
_Ramon Llull's Thinking Machine_, at first admitting freely and
unperturbed by its intricate futility.[^3]

> [M]easured against its objective, judged by its inventor's
> illustrious goal, the thinking machine does not work. For us, that
> fact is of secondary importance. The perpetual motion machines
> depicted in sketches that confer their mystery upon the pages of the
> most effusive encyclopedias don't work either, nor do the
> metaphysical and theological theories that customarily declare who
> we are and what manner of thing the world is. Their public and
> well-known futility does not diminish their interest.

Borges goes on to offer us an anemic defense of Llull's _Ars_.
After acknowledging the poverty of its revelations and the undeserving
locus of its laborious construction, Borges corrects himself: "I said
that the thinking machine does not work. I have slandered it: _elle ne
fonctionne que trop_, it works all too well." The mechanism produces
an embarrassment of possible truths, each undifferentiated from the
next, a "torrential ambiguity."  He concludes (rather weakly) that
Llull's thinking machine may enjoy some utility as a "literary and
poetic device."

The circumstances of Llull's own life are of not so much contemporary
interest, but briefly, Llull is moved by a vision of Christ to:[^4]

> ... accept the possibility of martyrdom in an attempt to missionize;
> to try “to write a book, the best in the world, against the errors
> of the unbelievers”; and “to go to the pope, to kings, and to
> Christian princes to incite them” to establish language monasteries
> for missionaries.

After nearly a decade of study and solitude, another divine revelation
presented to him the "form and method" of the _Ars_, a combinatorial
system for asking and answering questions that he would continually
develop, refine, streamline, and explicate in over 250 works written
between 1274-1316. The nebulous power Llull's _Ars_ exerted over the
nascent sciences would persist for five centuries; Agrippa, Bruno, and
Leibniz were all influenced by him. Where the _Ars_ formed the bedrock
of Llull's Christian apologetics, it also functioned more generally
(not unlike the dialectic) as a universal methodology, a practical art
of investigation for all subjects.

---

Returning now to _A Note on (toward) Bernard Shaw_, Borges identifies
a pathology more severe underlying the logic of the _Ars_:

> Lully's machine... can be the subject of jokes, but [it]
> exaggerate[s] a propension which is common: making metaphysics and
> the arts into a kind of play with combinations.

It is (regrettably, dismally) in vogue these days to suggest that the
discovery of truths in the mathematical and natural sciences are more
amenable to this approach. (A discussion of whether this future is a
_desirable_ one appears to be beyond us at the moment. For now, we are
focused too much on its inevitability.) We read on:

> Those who practice this game forget that a book is more than a
> verbal structure or series of verbal structures; it is the dialogue
> it establishes with its reader and the intonation it imposes upon
> his voice and the changing and durable images it leaves in his
> memory. This dialogue is infinite... Literature is not exhaustible,
> for the sufficient and simple reason that no single book is. A book
> is not an isolated being: it is a relationship, an axis of
> innumerable relationships.

I feel there is an argument here that the same applies to mathematics
and the natural sciences, but it is not clear to me yet.
